01. Sales are growing. Why isn't profit?

Sales can rise while the business is less healthy. The project and product data you already keep show which work pays, and which work costs more than it returns.

02. Which clients, projects or products are worth growing?

Not every client or product deserves more of the business. Compare what each one brings in with what it takes to deliver, using your own data.

03. Where are we spending more than the work is worth?

Look at delivery time, direct costs and the running costs already in your reports. See which spend earns its place, and which you can reduce.

04. Can we take on more work, or is the team already full?

Match the work you want with the hours you have. Your capacity data show whether the next yes needs a higher price, a later start, or more people.

05. Why does cash feel tight when everyone is busy?

Busy is not the same as cash in the bank. Your invoices and payment dates show work you have delivered and not yet been paid for. Cash is one part of the picture.

What happened

Three projects generated $50,000 in sales and cost $38,000 to deliver, leaving $12,000.

Why it matters

That is $24 per $100 sold, before rent, other running costs and tax.

What to check

Before setting the next price, check that every delivery cost is included and whether the amount left covers the remaining running costs.

### Common questions

Does the $12,000 mean profit?

It is the amount left after the costs of doing these projects. Rent, other shared running costs, interest and tax still have to come out of it. You need those figures before you can calculate final profit.

Which projects leave more?

Project A leaves $6,000 from $20,000 in sales: $30 per $100. Project B leaves $3,600 from $18,000, and Project C leaves $2,400 from $12,000: $20 per $100 each. Their prices and delivery costs need checking; these figures do not tell us why they differ.

What should I check before the next quote?

Check that the estimate includes every cost of doing the work. Compare the price and cost breakdown for Projects B and C with Project A. In the extra-cost example below, another $5,000 of costs cuts the amount left from $12,000 to $7,000.

### See the numbers and assumptions

All examples use fictional US dollars (USD), not service prices or currency conversions.

Table: Sales and delivery costs for three fictional projects

| Project | Sales | Delivery costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Project A | $20,000 | $14,000 |
| Project B | $18,000 | $14,400 |
| Project C | $12,000 | $9,600 |

Delivery costs are the costs assigned to doing these projects. Rent, other shared running costs, interest and tax are excluded.

What if the same work costs $5,000 more?

Keep sales at $50,000. Increase delivery costs from $38,000 to $43,000. The amount left falls from $12,000 to $7,000.

Table: What if the same work costs $5,000 more?

| Case | Sales | Delivery costs | Left after delivery costs | Percentage of sales left |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Current example | $50,000 | $38,000 | $12,000 | 24% |
| $5,000 more delivery costs | $50,000 | $43,000 | $7,000 | 14% |

Only delivery costs change in this example. Rent, other running costs and tax still have to be covered.

#### Check the calculations

- Total sales: $20,000 + $18,000 + $12,000 = $50,000.
- Total delivery costs: $14,000 + $14,400 + $9,600 = $38,000.
- Left after delivery: $50,000 − $38,000 = $12,000. Divide $12,000 by $50,000 to get 24%, or $24 left per $100 sold.
- Project A leaves $6,000 of $20,000, or $30 per $100 sold. Project B leaves $3,600 of $18,000, and Project C leaves $2,400 of $12,000: each leaves $20 per $100 sold.
- With the extra cost: $50,000 − $43,000 = $7,000. Divide $7,000 by $50,000 to get 14%, or $14 left per $100 sold.

### See the details

Summary: See the details

Beside the summary: Numbers, assumptions and scope

What happened

A $40,000 customer payment arriving ten days late creates a $10,000 shortfall on 20 November.

Why it matters

The final $35,000 balance cannot pay a bill that was due earlier.

What to check

Confirm the customer’s payment date and how the $10,000 gap would be covered before the supplier bill is due.

### Common questions

Why does a late payment matter if the month ends with $35,000?

On 20 November, a $25,000 supplier bill is due. Without the $40,000 customer payment, only $15,000 is available. The business needs another $10,000 that day, even though later payments bring the final balance back to $35,000.

What does −$10,000 on the chart mean?

It means the listed bills exceed the cash available by $10,000 on 20 November. It is a funding gap to resolve, not an approved overdraft. The chart checks each payment date, so a positive final balance cannot hide an earlier gap.

What should I check before the bill is due?

Confirm when the customer will pay and whether the supplier’s 20 November due date is fixed. Check any cash already available for the gap. If the payment date needs to change, agree that with the supplier in advance.

- After that shortfall: −$10,000 + $25,000 received on 23 November + $40,000 received on 26 November − $20,000 paid on 27 November = $35,000.

### Common questions

How does the $23,000 gap add up?

Sales were $15,000 below plan. Payroll was $6,000 above plan, and delivery costs were $2,000 above plan. All three reduce the amount left: $15,000 + $6,000 + $2,000 = $23,000. Rent matched the plan.

Does this tell us why sales fell or costs rose?

No. It shows where the differences are. Compare actual orders with the sales plan, payroll records with the staffing plan, and supplier bills with the delivery-cost budget. Those records are needed to explain the changes.

Which difference should I look at first?

Start with the $15,000 sales gap because it is the largest. Then investigate the $6,000 extra payroll and $2,000 extra delivery costs. Check whether each difference is temporary or should change the next budget.

Why is profit different from money in the bank?

The accounts include $120,000 of sales, but customers have paid only $80,000. The business also paid $20,000 for stock it has not sold yet. Sales can count before payment arrives, and buying unsold stock can use cash before its cost appears in profit.

Profit already deducts $60,000 for the items sold, but only $45,000 has been paid to suppliers. Add back the unpaid $15,000 when explaining the cash change because that money has not left the bank yet. It is still owed; it is not extra income.

How does the full cash calculation work?

Start with the $30,000 listed profit. Subtract $40,000 of sales not yet collected, add $15,000 of supplier costs not yet paid, then subtract $20,000 spent on extra stock. The result is a $15,000 cash decrease. Check the unpaid invoices and supplier due dates, and whether the extra stock is needed.
